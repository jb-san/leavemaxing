A four-day weekend is the most common break the planner finds: a Thursday holiday plus a Friday bridge day, or a Friday-to-Monday Easter. Four days is long enough to go somewhere properly and short enough to waste if the logistics go wrong. This is the system we suggest, from choosing a destination to closing the bag.

## Start with the time budget

Four days off is three nights. Of those days, travel eats more than people expect: getting to the airport, security, the flight, the transfer, finding the accommodation. A rough rule that works:

- **Up to about three hours door to door each way:** you get three and a half real days. Ideal.
- **Four to five hours each way:** fine if you leave the evening before or very early on the first day.
- **More than five hours each way:** you've turned the trip into two days in the middle. Save that destination for a longer break.

Trains count differently from flights. Five hours on a train from city centre to city centre, with a seat and a table, is often less tiring than three hours of flying once you add the airport at each end. For trips within Europe, compare the door-to-door time, not the flight time.

## When to leave and when to come back

The best four-day trips start on the evening before the first day off, or early on the first morning, and end in the afternoon of the last day. Some rules of thumb:

- **Arrive by lunch on day one.** Then the first day counts.
- **Don't plan anything important on the morning you leave.** If the outbound is delayed, a booked tour or restaurant on the first morning is the first thing you lose.
- **Return in the afternoon of the last day, not late in the evening.** You'll be back at work the next day, and evening returns on holiday weekends are the most delay-prone and the busiest.

## What to book in advance, and what not to

For a short trip, the main risk isn't paying too much, it's losing half a day to a queue or a sold-out ticket. Book:

1. **Transport there and back,** including the train to the airport if you rely on an early one.
2. **The accommodation,** close to where you'll spend your time. On a short trip, a central location is worth more than a nicer room further out.
3. **Timed tickets for the one or two sights you care most about.** Many famous sights in Europe now require timed tickets bought online, and they sell out on holiday weekends: the Anne Frank House in Amsterdam, the Sagrada Família in Barcelona, the Alhambra in Granada, the Last Supper in Milan and the Vatican Museums, for example.
4. **One dinner,** for the busiest evening, usually Saturday.

Don't book a full itinerary. On four days, two fixed points a day at most leaves room for the things you discover.

## Watch out for whose holiday it is

Your holiday isn't necessarily theirs, and theirs isn't necessarily yours. This works both ways:

- **Ascension, Whit Monday and Corpus Christi** are working days in some countries (Spain, Italy and Greece have Ascension as a normal day, for example), so shops and museums there keep normal hours while you're on holiday.
- **Easter Monday and Whit Monday** are holidays in much of Europe, so on those days expect closed shops and local crowds at the sights.
- **Sundays** are still quiet in Germany and Austria, with most shops closed.

Check the opening hours of anything important for the exact days you're there.

## The carry-on packing list

Four days fits in a cabin bag, and with most low-cost airlines in the free under-seat bag if you're disciplined. Carry-on only means no waiting at the luggage belt and nothing lost on a tight connection.

**Clothes (for a temperate climate):**

- What you travel in: your bulkiest shoes, trousers and jacket.
- One more pair of trousers or a skirt.
- Three tops, one of which works for dinner.
- One warm layer (a jumper or fleece).
- A light rain jacket, unless your travel jacket is waterproof.
- Underwear and socks for four days, or three and wash one set.
- Swimwear if there's any chance of a pool, sauna or lake. It weighs nothing.

**Everything else:**

- Phone, charger and a short cable, plus a travel adaptor if you're going to the UK, Ireland, Malta or Cyprus.
- A small power bank, carried in the cabin (lithium batteries aren't allowed in checked bags).
- Liquids in containers of up to 100 ml in one transparent bag. A few airports with new scanners have relaxed this, but you can't count on it on the way home, so pack for the strictest airport on your trip.
- Medicines, in the cabin bag, with any prescriptions.
- Earplugs and an eye mask for thin hotel walls and early flights.
- A tote bag for the day.

Leave the "just in case" items at home. On a four-day city break, almost anything you've forgotten can be bought.

## Documents and money

- **ID.** Within the Schengen area, an EU citizen can travel with a national ID card as well as a passport. Check the expiry date when you book, not the night before.
- **The UK now needs an ETA.** Since 2025, EU citizens need an electronic travel authorisation before visiting the UK. Apply online in good time.
- **Health insurance.** Carry the European Health Insurance Card, which in Germany is on the back of most health insurance cards and in Sweden is ordered free from Försäkringskassan. It doesn't replace travel insurance.
- **Cards.** Bring two cards, kept in different places, and pay in the local currency when asked.

## An evening's work

Once the dates are approved, a four-day trip can be booked in one evening: transport, accommodation, the one ticket that matters, one dinner. Pack the night before with the list above, and the only thing left to plan is the trip itself.
