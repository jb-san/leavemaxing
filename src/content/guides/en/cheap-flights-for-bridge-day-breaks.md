The problem with bridge-day breaks is that they're the same for everyone. When Ascension falls on a Thursday, half the country takes Friday off and tries to fly on Wednesday evening, and airlines know it. You can't move the holiday, but you have more control over the price than it seems. This is what actually helps, in rough order of how much it usually saves.

## 1. Change the shape of the break, not just the dates

Most people search the obvious version of a break: leave on the evening before the holiday, return on the last evening. Those are exactly the flights that sell out first. A few alternatives that use the same number of leave days, or one more:

- **Fly on the holiday itself.** Thursday morning instead of Wednesday evening at Ascension, Christmas Day instead of the 23rd, New Year's Day instead of the 30th. Holiday mornings are often among the cheapest departures of the whole period, because people want to be at home or already away.
- **Come back a day early, or a day late.** Sunday evening is the peak return after almost every long weekend. Sunday morning, or Monday morning with one extra leave day, is often cheaper, and sometimes by enough to pay for that day.
- **Put the leave on the other side.** Many breaks can be built either before or after a holiday. Easter 2027 in Germany, for example, gives ten days for four leave days either from 20 to 29 March or from 26 March to 4 April. One of the two is usually busier, depending on school holidays.

The planner shows the different ways to spend your leave around each holiday. Try a few shapes in the planner for [Sweden 2027](/se/2027/) or [Germany 2027](/de/2027/), then price the flights for each one before you commit to a set of dates with your manager.

## 2. Be flexible on where, not when

For a bridge day, your dates are fixed by the calendar. Your destination usually isn't. Every big flight search has a mode without a destination, which turns the question around: given these dates, where can I go?

- Kiwi.com's search accepts "anywhere" as the destination and lists the cheapest places first.
- Google Flights has Explore, a map of prices from your airport for your dates.
- Skyscanner has a search to "Everywhere".

Start broad, then narrow it down: within three hours, then by the type of trip you want. Destinations with lots of competition between airlines on the route are usually cheaper than ones served by a single carrier.

## 3. Check the other airports, and count the transfer

Low-cost routes often leave from secondary airports. In Sweden, Skavsta and Västerås are both well over an hour from Stockholm, and from Skåne, Copenhagen Airport usually has far more direct routes than Malmö. In Germany, Frankfurt-Hahn, Weeze ("Düsseldorf Weeze") and Memmingen ("Munich West") are all far from the cities in their names, while Basel, Salzburg and Strasbourg can be useful from the south-west and south.

Always count the full cost: the airport bus or train, the time, and whether the first train of the day gets you there for an early flight. A cheaper ticket from a distant airport that needs a hotel the night before isn't cheaper.

## 4. Book when the price is reasonable, not when it's perfect

There's no reliable magic day of the week to book, and no exact number of weeks before departure that always wins. What is reliable for holiday weekends is that the cheapest fares on the popular departures go first, and prices for those departures tend to rise as the date gets closer. Last-minute deals do exist, but mostly on routes and dates with spare seats, which bridge-day weekends usually aren't.

A practical approach:

1. Decide on your dates as soon as your leave is approved. For Easter, Ascension and Christmas that often means months ahead.
2. Set a price alert for two or three options (different airports, the different shapes of the break).
3. Book when a price looks fair for that trip. Watching for weeks in the hope of a drop usually ends with the good departures sold out.

Clearing cookies or browsing in private mode to get lower prices is a popular tip; we haven't seen good evidence that it matters. Price differences between searches usually reflect fares selling out or the currency and site you used.

## 5. Compare the real price, with bags and seats

Low-cost carriers sell a seat; everything else costs extra. For a four-day trip you can usually manage with a small under-seat bag, but each airline defines that bag differently, and the fees for a larger cabin bag or a checked bag can double the price of a cheap ticket. Before you compare two flights:

- Check the size of the free bag on each airline, and measure your bag.
- Add the price of the bags you actually need, in both directions.
- Skip seat selection unless you're travelling together and care about sitting together; even then, check whether the airline seats families together for free.

With bags included, a network airline is sometimes cheaper than a low-cost one, especially on routes where both compete.

## 6. Know what you're buying with combined tickets

Search sites like Kiwi.com sometimes combine flights from different airlines on separate tickets to make a cheaper route. That can be a good deal, but understand it: if the first flight is late and you miss the second, the second airline doesn't have to help you, unless the site you booked with offers its own guarantee. Give yourself plenty of time between flights, avoid the last connection of the day, and don't use separate tickets when you have to be back at work the next morning.

## 7. Packages can win in peak weeks

In Sweden especially, package holidays (charter flights plus hotel) can be cheaper than building the same trip yourself in peak periods, because tour operators buy the seats and rooms in bulk. For a beach week in the Canaries during sportlov or a Christmas trip, compare a package with flights plus hotel before booking either.

## 8. Know your rights if things go wrong

For flights departing from an EU airport, and EU airline flights into the EU, EU Regulation 261/2004 gives you a right to care (meals, and a hotel if needed) during long delays and cancellations, and in many cases compensation. The rules are detailed, and airlines don't always volunteer them. If your bridge weekend is ruined by a cancellation, read the rules on the EU's Your Europe site before you accept a voucher.

## 9. Pay in the local currency

When a card machine or booking site abroad offers to charge you in your home currency, decline and pay in the local currency. The conversion offered at the point of sale is usually worse than your card's own rate.

## A short checklist

- Price at least two shapes of the break, including one where you fly on the holiday itself.
- Search "anywhere" for your dates before picking a destination.
- Compare airports with the transfer included.
- Compare prices with the bags you'll actually take.
- Book when the price is fair, then stop looking.
