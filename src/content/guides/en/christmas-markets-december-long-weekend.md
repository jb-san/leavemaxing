December has no public holidays before Christmas in Germany or Sweden, so a Christmas market trip costs leave. The good news is that it doesn't cost much: one leave day on a Friday or Monday makes a three-day weekend, and two make four days, which is plenty for one city and its markets. This guide covers the classic markets, when they usually run, and which weekends to pick in December 2026.

## The Advent weekends in 2026

Christmas Day 2026 is a Friday, so the four Sundays of Advent are 29 November and 6, 13 and 20 December. Most big markets open in the last week of November and run until 23 December, with some lasting until Christmas Eve or beyond.

| Weekend | With Friday off | With Thursday and Friday off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st Advent | Fri 27 – Sun 29 Nov | Thu 26 – Sun 29 Nov |
| 2nd Advent | Fri 4 – Sun 6 Dec | Thu 3 – Sun 6 Dec |
| 3rd Advent | Fri 11 – Sun 13 Dec | Thu 10 – Sun 13 Dec |
| 4th Advent | Fri 18 – Sun 20 Dec | Thu 17 – Sun 20 Dec |

Our advice:

- **The second and third weekends** are the sweet spot. Every market is open, and the crowds are lighter than on the last weekend before Christmas.
- **Go Thursday to Sunday, not Friday to Monday.** Thursday evenings at the markets are busy with locals but manageable; Saturday afternoons and evenings are the most crowded time of the week almost everywhere. Arriving on Thursday gives you a quiet Friday morning.
- **Avoid the opening weekend in the most famous markets** if crowds bother you. Opening ceremonies are a spectacle but draw the biggest numbers.

For next year: in 2027, Christmas Day is a Saturday, so the Advent Sundays are 28 November and 5, 12 and 19 December 2027. In Germany both Christmas holidays fall on the weekend in 2027, and Christmas Eve and New Year's Eve are Fridays, which is worth knowing when you plan your 2027 leave in the [planner for Germany](/de/2027/) or [Sweden](/se/2027/).

## The classic German markets

Dates below are what these markets usually do; each one publishes its own dates for the year, so check before you book.

**Nuremberg (Christkindlesmarkt).** The most famous of them all, on the main market square. It traditionally opens on the Friday before the first Sunday of Advent with the Christkind's prologue from the church balcony, which in 2026 would be Friday 27 November, and runs until Christmas Eve. Try Nürnberger Rostbratwurst (three small sausages in a roll), Lebkuchen and the red plum figures called Zwetschgenmännle. The city fills up on weekends; book a hotel early, or stay in nearby Fürth or Erlangen, both a short train ride away.

**Dresden (Striezelmarkt).** One of the oldest Christmas markets in Germany, first held in the 15th century, on the Altmarkt. Dresden is a market city: there are several other markets in the old town, including a medieval one in the Stallhof. Try the Dresdner Christstollen. It usually runs from late November to Christmas Eve.

**Cologne.** Not one market but several, each with its own character: below the cathedral, on the Alter Markt, the "angels' market" on Neumarkt and a harbour market by the chocolate museum. They usually run from mid- or late November until 23 December. Cologne is easy by train from much of Germany, the Netherlands and Belgium, which also makes it busy.

**Smaller and atmospheric:** the Reiterlesmarkt in Rothenburg ob der Tauber, the medieval market in Esslingen, the market in the courtyard of Thurn und Taxis Palace in Regensburg (with an entry fee), Lübeck's markets around the town hall and the old merchant houses, and Aachen around the cathedral.

## Elsewhere in Europe

**Vienna.** The biggest market is in front of the City Hall (Rathausplatz), and the ones at Spittelberg, Karlsplatz and Schönbrunn Palace are smaller and calmer. Vienna's markets often open in mid-November, earlier than Germany's, and several run until 26 December. In Austria, 8 December (the Immaculate Conception) is a public holiday, but many shops open and the markets run as normal, so expect a busy day.

**Strasbourg.** The city calls itself the "Capital of Christmas", with markets spread across the old town island, and they usually run from late November until 24 December. Expect security checks at the entrances to the island and very busy weekends.

**Prague.** The markets on the Old Town Square and Wenceslas Square usually open at the end of November and run into early January, so they also suit a trip between Christmas and New Year.

**Budapest.** The market on Vörösmarty Square and the one in front of St Stephen's Basilica, with a light show on the façade, usually run from mid-November until around New Year.

**Copenhagen.** Tivoli's Christmas season, usually from mid-November into the first days of January, is the main draw, along with the market at Nyhavn.

**Stockholm and Gothenburg.** Skansen holds a Christmas market on the Advent weekends, and the market on Stortorget in Gamla stan usually runs from late November until just before Christmas. In Gothenburg, Liseberg's Christmas market and lights are the big attraction. Swedish markets are smaller than German ones, but glögg, saffron buns and the dark afternoons make up for it.

## Getting there

From most of Germany, the train is the obvious choice: Nuremberg, Dresden, Cologne and Strasbourg all have big stations a short walk from the markets. Book early in December, especially for Friday afternoons and Sunday evenings.

From Sweden, fly to the city rather than the region: Stockholm and Gothenburg have direct flights to Vienna, Prague, Berlin, Munich, Frankfurt and Hamburg, and from Munich Nuremberg is about an hour by train, from Berlin Dresden about two hours.

## Practical tips

1. **Mugs come with a deposit.** At German and Austrian markets, glühwein comes in a mug with a deposit (Pfand) of a few euros. Return it for the money or keep it as a souvenir; many markets have a new design every year.
2. **Bring some cash.** More stalls take cards every year, but not all, especially at smaller markets.
3. **Dress for standing still.** You'll spend hours outside, mostly standing. Warm shoes and a hat matter more than a fashionable coat.
4. **Go at dusk.** The markets are at their best when the lights come on, which in December is around 4 pm in Germany and earlier in the Nordics. Spend the morning in a museum and the evening at the markets.
5. **Eat at the stalls, but book one dinner.** Restaurants near the big markets are full on weekend evenings; book a table for the Saturday.

## Our pick

For a first trip: Nuremberg or Dresden on a Thursday to Sunday in the second or third week of Advent, one or two leave days, by train. From Sweden: Vienna, with a direct flight, the Rathausplatz market and an evening at a coffee house.
