Midsummer is the Swedish holiday that Swedes themselves rate highest: a long light evening, herring and new potatoes, strawberries, a maypole and dancing that people pretend to find embarrassing. In 2027, **Midsummer Eve (midsommarafton) is Friday 25 June** and **Midsummer Day (midsommardagen) is Saturday 26 June**. Midsummer always falls on the Friday and Saturday between 19 and 26 June, and 2027 is as late as it can be.

## The dates and the leave they take

Midsummer Day, the Saturday, is the official public holiday. Midsummer Eve isn't one by law, but it's a de facto day off for almost everyone: most employers close, and our planner treats it as a day off. So the base break is three days from Friday to Sunday, without any leave.

The four days before Midsummer (21–24 June) are the best value: four leave days for nine days off. The week after starts the main holiday season for many people, and Swedish law gives employees the right to four consecutive weeks of leave in June, July or August unless they agree otherwise, so the Midsummer week is often where the summer holiday begins.

## What the day looks like

If you've never been to a Swedish Midsummer, it helps to know the shape of the day, because most celebrations follow it closely.

- **Late morning or early afternoon:** people gather on a meadow or village green to dress the maypole (midsommarstång) with birch leaves and flowers, and many pick flowers for the wreaths worn in the hair.
- **Afternoon:** the pole is raised, by hand at the traditional celebrations, and the dancing begins in rings around it, led by a fiddler or an accordion. Children are the most enthusiastic dancers; adults join in, with varying levels of irony.
- **Evening:** dinner, usually outdoors if the weather allows, with several kinds of pickled herring, new potatoes, sour cream, chives, often salmon, and strawberries with cream for dessert. Snaps comes with songs (snapsvisor), and a printed song sheet is normal.
- **Late evening:** the light barely fades in the south and not at all in the north. An old tradition says that if you pick seven kinds of flowers on Midsummer night and put them under your pillow, you'll dream of the person you'll marry.

Public celebrations are free and open to everyone, and you don't need to dress up, although wreaths are welcome. Bring something to sit on, and a jumper for the evening, whatever the forecast says.

## Where to celebrate

Midsummer is celebrated almost everywhere in Sweden, so you don't need to go far. But some places do it in a way worth travelling for.

### Dalarna: the traditional version

Around Lake Siljan (Leksand, Rättvik, Tällberg, Mora), Midsummer is celebrated with folk costume, fiddle music and the raising of big decorated maypoles in front of large crowds. Leksand's celebration is one of the best known in the country. It's about three hours from Stockholm by train or car. Accommodation around Siljan sells out well ahead of Midsummer, so if you want to stay there for the weekend, book in winter.

### The Stockholm archipelago

For many people in Stockholm, Midsummer means an island: Grinda, Utö, Sandhamn, Möja or one of the many smaller islands. Waxholmsbolaget boats and other ferries leave from Strömkajen in the city centre and from harbours further out. The summer timetable normally starts in June, and the boats on Midsummer Eve morning are crowded; book where you can, and arrive early where you can't. Hostels, guesthouses and camping on the islands fill up early.

### Skansen in Stockholm

If you're in Stockholm without a cottage, the open-air museum Skansen holds a traditional celebration with maypole dancing over the Midsummer weekend. It's busy with visitors from abroad and families, and it's the easiest way to see the traditions without knowing anyone.

### Gotland

The ferry from Nynäshamn to Visby takes around three hours, and Gotland at Midsummer is a mix of traditional celebrations in the villages and a busy start to the summer season in Visby. The ferry over Midsummer sells out, especially for cars, so book as soon as you can.

### The west coast

Bohuslän (Marstrand, Smögen, Fjällbacka, the islands north of Gothenburg) combines Midsummer with the classic west coast: granite rocks, seafood and bathing, if you're brave; the sea at the end of June is usually still cool, often below 20 °C. The towns are full and lively on Midsummer weekend.

### The north: the midnight sun

North of the Arctic Circle, the sun doesn't set at all around Midsummer. In Kiruna and Abisko you get it for several weeks either side. Midsummer in the mountains is quieter than in the south, but it's an extraordinary time to walk: the Kungsleden trail starts at Abisko, and the mountain stations of the Swedish Tourist Association (STF) open for the summer around this time. Snow can still lie on the higher passes in late June, so check conditions.

## Practical tips

1. **Systembolaget is closed from Midsummer Eve until Monday.** The state alcohol shops don't open on Midsummer Eve or over the weekend, and the Thursday queues are long. Shop on Wednesday or earlier.
2. **Shops have shorter hours.** Supermarkets are usually open on Midsummer Eve, with shorter hours, and some close on Midsummer Day. Buy what you need for the weekend in advance.
3. **Travel on Thursday, or early Friday.** The roads out of Stockholm, Gothenburg and Malmö are at their busiest on Thursday afternoon and Friday morning, and trains on those days sell out. Coming back, Sunday afternoon is the peak.
4. **Plan for any weather.** Midsummer weather is famously unreliable: anything from 25 °C and sun to 12 °C and rain. Bring a warm jumper and a rain jacket, and plan a dry alternative.
5. **Bring mosquito repellent.** Especially by lakes, in the forest and in the north.
6. **If you go abroad instead, go on Saturday.** Some Swedes use Midsummer to fly south. Friday departures are expensive; Saturday or the following Monday are often cheaper.

## For visitors from abroad

If you're visiting Sweden at Midsummer, know that the cities empty out: many Swedes go to summer houses or the countryside. Stockholm is pleasant and quieter, but some restaurants and shops close for the weekend. The best way to experience Midsummer is with Swedes, either at a public celebration like Skansen or Leksand, or by accepting any invitation you get. The food (pickled herring, new potatoes with dill, sour cream and chives, and strawberry cake) and the songs are a big part of it, and so is the dance around the maypole, usually to "Små grodorna" (the little frogs), which involves hopping.

## Our pick

Take 21–24 June off for nine days. Spend the first part of the week somewhere quiet, such as the archipelago or the mountains, and finish at a real celebration on the Friday: Dalarna if you want tradition, an island if you want the Stockholm version.
